[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 1 - Section 1.1: Chloroplasts & Pigments]
Chloroplasts are the primary photosynthetic organelles in plant cells. They contain disc-like structures called thylakoids arranged in stacks termed grana. The fluid surrounding the thylakoids is the stroma. Chlorophyll a is the primary photosynthetic pigment, absorbing blue-violet and red light while reflecting green. Accessory pigments like chlorophyll b and carotenoids absorb additional wavelengths and protect the chloroplast against photo-damage.

[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 1 - Section 1.2: Light-Dependent Reactions]
The light reactions take place on the thylakoid membrane. Light energy strikes Photosystem II (P680), exciting electrons that are transferred along an electron transport chain to Photosystem I (P700). Photolysis of water occurs at PSII, splitting water into protons, electrons, and oxygen gas (O2). As electrons move through cytochrome complexes, protons are pumped into the thylakoid lumen, creating an electrochemical gradient. Chemiosmosis drives ATP synthase to generate ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. Photosystem I re-energizes electrons to reduce NADP+ to NADPH via NADP+ reductase.

[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 1 - Section 1.3: The Calvin Cycle]
The Calvin Cycle takes place in the stroma and does not directly require light, but depends on ATP and NADPH from the light reactions. Carbon fixation is catalyzed by the enzyme RuBisCO (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase), which binds CO2 to ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP) to form 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). ATP and NADPH reduce 3-PGA to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P). For every six turns of the cycle, two molecules of G3P exit to form one glucose molecule, while the remaining G3P molecules use ATP to regenerate RuBP.

[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 2 - Section 2.1: Glycolysis]
Glycolysis is an anaerobic process taking place entirely in the cytosol. A single molecule of six-carbon glucose is enzymatically converted into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules. The pathway involves an energy-investment phase requiring 2 ATP, followed by an energy-payoff phase yielding 4 ATP via substrate-level phosphorylation and 2 molecules of NADH. Thus, the net yield per glucose molecule is 2 ATP, 2 NADH, and 2 pyruvate. Oxygen is not directly consumed in glycolysis.

[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 2 - Section 2.2: The Krebs Cycle]
In the presence of oxygen, pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix where it undergoes oxidative decarboxylation to form Acetyl-CoA, releasing CO2 and producing 1 NADH. Acetyl-CoA joins with four-carbon oxaloacetate to form six-carbon citrate, starting the Krebs cycle. Over one full cycle per Acetyl-CoA (two cycles per glucose), the cycle releases 2 CO2, produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, and 1 ATP (or GTP). Oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end of the cycle to combine with another Acetyl-CoA.

[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 2 - Section 2.3: Oxidative Phosphorylation & Chemiosmosis]
Oxidative phosphorylation occurs at the inner mitochondrial cristae membrane. High-energy electrons donated by NADH and FADH2 pass through four protein complexes (I, II, III, and IV). As electrons flow down the chain toward molecular oxygen (the terminal electron acceptor, forming H2O), Complexes I, III, and IV pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space. The steep proton gradient drives protons back through the rotor of ATP Synthase into the matrix, synthesizing approximately 26 to 28 ATP per glucose molecule by chemiosmosis.

[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 3 - Section 3.1: DNA Structure]
DNA is a double-stranded helical polymer of nucleotides. Each nucleotide consists of a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base. The two strands run antiparallel (5' to 3' and 3' to 5') and are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs: Adenine pairs with Thymine (forming 2 hydrogen bonds), and Guanine pairs with Cytosine (forming 3 hydrogen bonds). The outer backbone consists of alternating sugar and phosphate groups linked by phosphodiester bonds.

[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 3 - Section 3.2: DNA Replication Machinery]
DNA replication is semiconservative. Helicase unwinds the double helix at the replication fork, while single-stranded binding proteins (SSBs) prevent premature re-annealing. Topoisomerase relieves torsional strain. Primase synthesizes short RNA primers required for DNA Polymerase III to initiate synthesis. DNA Polymerase III synthesizes DNA continuously along the leading strand in the 5' to 3' direction. On the lagging strand, DNA is synthesized discontinuously in short segments known as Okazaki fragments. DNA Polymerase I removes RNA primers and fills gaps with DNA nucleotides, and DNA Ligase seals the nicks in the phosphodiester backbone.

[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 4 - Section 4.1: Transcription]
Transcription occurs in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells. RNA Polymerase binds to a specific promoter region (such as the TATA box) on the DNA template strand and synthesizes a complementary pre-mRNA molecule in the 5' to 3' direction. In eukaryotes, the pre-mRNA undergoes three crucial post-transcriptional modifications before nuclear export: addition of a 5' methyl-guanosine cap, addition of a 3' poly-A tail, and splicing carried out by spliceosomes to remove non-coding introns and join coding exons.

[SOURCE: Textbook Ch. 4 - Section 4.2: Translation & Protein Assembly]
Translation occurs in the cytoplasm on ribosomes (composed of rRNA and ribosomal proteins). The small ribosomal subunit binds to mRNA at the start codon (AUG, coding for methionine). Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules carrying specific amino acids align with codons through complementary three-base anticodons. The ribosome has three active sites: A (aminoacyl), P (peptidyl), and E (exit). Peptide bonds are catalyzed between amino acids on the P and A sites. Elongation continues until a stop codon (UAA, UAG, or UGA) reaches the A site, recruiting a release factor that frees the newly synthesized polypeptide chain.
